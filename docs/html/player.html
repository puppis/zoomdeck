The player

Keyboard and pointer controls, the pen and its pressure styles, the whiteboard, a companion tablet, the camera, recording a talk and exporting it as a video, printing the deck to a PDF, spotlight and laser, reverse playback, deep links, speed and fullscreen.

Open in the tour ↗

Controls#

Default keys Action
→, Space next stop (plays its animations)
← previous stop, playing the current stop backwards
Home first stop
A replay the current stop (play it again)
⌘Z / Ctrl+Z undo the last pen or highlighter stroke
⌘⇧Z / Ctrl+Shift+Z / Ctrl+Y redo a stroke
Delete, Backspace clear all strokes on this slide
D drawing on or off: while on, a plain drag draws and the pointer shows the pen in its color
1 … 4 pen color (Default, Red, Blue, Green): drawing on in that color, or off again when it is the color in use
L switch the pointer tool between spotlight and laser
W show or hide the whiteboard; while it is shown the arrows scroll it (their own entries in the settings)
C camera: in a corner, then full frame, then off
S film the screen for the time spent in another application, or stop; the browser asks which screen the first time
R record the talk (a new one, or continuing a saved one), or stop recording
E open the source line of this stop in the editor (preview server with --editor; see Editors)
P, ⌘P / Ctrl+P print the deck to a PDF, one page per stage
F toggle fullscreen; Esc leaves it
O toggle the slide outline
, open the settings

The footer has four groups: the previous/next buttons with the stop counter; the pen colors and the Whiteboard button; the Camera, Screen, Record, Pause and Talk buttons; and, at the right, Print, Fullscreen and the settings gear. A progress bar runs along its top edge. Camera, Screen and Record turn red while they are at work. The header shows the breadcrumb of the current slide and the state: the player's (Playing, Paused, Rewinding), then whatever is on, as in "Paused | Whiteboard | Camera | Recording". In fullscreen, where header and footer are hidden, a dot in the top right corner of the slides stands in: green while the camera is on, red while the screen is filmed.

Settings#

The gear button (or the , key) opens the settings dialog, in six parts:

  • Pointer: the tool shown by the pointer gesture (spotlight or laser) and the modifier keys of each gesture: the pen, the spotlight/highlighter, click-to-advance and the click that opens an element's source in the editor (Alt by default). Each can be "Plain" (no key), one of Shift, Control, Alt, ⌘/Win or a pair of them, or "Off"; choosing keys already used by another gesture frees them from it.
  • Pen: how pressure and motion shape a stroke; see The pen.
  • Recording: the microphone and camera, where the camera picture sits, and whether the screen is filmed while the page is hidden; see Recording a talk.
  • Tablet: pairing a companion tablet, the tablets connected and the trusted ones; see A tablet as a drawing pad.
  • Keyboard: every action with its keys, in three groups: the keys while presenting, the keys while the whiteboard is shown (the arrows that scroll it, which come before the first group then), and the keys while a recorded talk plays (play or pause, seeking, leaving; only those and fullscreen work then). Click a key to remove it, or choose "Add key" and press the new combination, which may include modifiers; a combination already used in the same group moves to the new action.
  • Advanced: the playback speed of the animations (0.5× to 2×), the rendering options (antialiasing and the render resolution, applied when the page loads again) and the reverse-playback switch, on by default.

Close at the bottom (or the × in the header, or Esc) closes the dialog; the settings are saved as they are changed.

"Restore default gestures", "Restore the default pen" and "Restore default keys" undo the changes of their section. Settings are stored in the browser and apply to every deck you open there.

Pointer tools#

With the default gestures:

  • Drag draws with the pen while drawing is on; a click leaves a dot. D turns drawing on or off: while on, the pointer over the slides is a pencil in the pen's color, the header says Pen, and links and live pages under the pointer are inert, so that a stroke can cross them; while off, the mouse only clicks and follows links, so that nothing is drawn by accident. Showing the whiteboard turns drawing on for as long as it is shown, and hiding it brings back what was before. The four colors in the footer (Default, which is the deck's text color, Red, Blue and Green) are toggle buttons for drawing: a color turns drawing on in that color, another color changes it, and the color in use, shown pressed with a pencil on it, turns drawing off again. The keys 1 to 4 do the same, which is how the color is changed in fullscreen. The tip size is in the Pen section of the settings. A stylus always draws, whatever the drawing switch says.

  • Hold Shift for a spotlight that follows the pointer, or a red laser dot: the L key (or the Pointer setting) switches between the two. Shift + scroll resizes either; Shift + drag draws a highlighter stroke.

  • A click never moves between stops unless you ask for it: the "Click: next stop" gesture is off by default, and binding it to a key (for example Alt) in the settings makes that key + click behave like the → key.

  • Alt + click an element to open its source line in your editor, when the deck is served by zoomdeck serve --editor … (the element nearest to the pointer counts, a text inside a tile before the tile); the E key opens the line of the current stop. See Editors.

  • Click a link to follow it, that is a press and release without movement: a Markdown link, or any element with href=, which is how the tour's front page and its "Back to the overview" lines work. Targets inside the deck (#slide=, #step=) move to that stop; others open in a new tab. The pointer turns into a hand over a link while drawing is off; while it is on, links are inert and the pencil stays. A double-click also follows a link inside the deck. An include is not a link by itself: the tour's front page lays an invisible rectangle with href= over each tile.

Strokes belong to the current slide and clear when the slide changes; undo, redo and clear act on the current slide's strokes (or on the whiteboard's while it is shown). Links and live pages keep working while drawing is off: clicking one opens it, and a stylus drag over one still draws; while drawing is on they are inert. On a touch screen a finger never draws, so a resting palm leaves no marks; drawing takes a pen, a stylus or the mouse. A fingertip rubbed over a stroke erases it, and undo brings it back where it was; two fingers scroll the whiteboard.

The pen#

A stroke is a path of samples with pressure, drawn as a filled shape whose width varies along it, the way drawing applications render ink. A pen with a pressure sensor (Apple Pencil, a Wacom tablet, a pen-enabled laptop) shapes the line as you press; with a mouse or a basic stylus the width follows the speed of the hand instead, so a quick flick ends thin. Samples are taken from the browser's raw pointer stream, before the frame is painted, which keeps the line close to the tip.

The Pen section of the settings holds the look:

Setting Effect
Tip size the width of a stroke at medium pressure: 1, 3, 6 or 10 px
Preset Ballpoint (light pressure effect), Fountain pen (strong, tapered), Marker (constant width), Brush (strongest); moving a slider makes it Custom
Pencil width width of a pencil stroke relative to the tip size, 0.2 to 1.5 (0.6 by default: a pencil draws finer than a mouse at the same tip); mouse strokes are not affected
Pencil pressure how much a real pressure sensor counts, 0.2 to 1.5 (0.6 by default, so a pencil draws about as light as a mouse); mouse strokes are not affected
Pressure effect how much pressure widens the line, from -1 (harder is thinner) to 1
Smoothing softening of the outline
Steadying how far the line lags behind the hand, which irons out jitter
Taper the ends thin the stroke in and out
Vary the width with speed pressure simulated from speed when the device reports none

Pencil strokes use half the steadying, since a pressure pen reports dense and precise positions, the line is extended with the browser's prediction of the pen's next positions so it stays under the tip, and fast strokes are drawn along a smooth curve through the samples, so quick handwriting keeps round curves. Changing a setting redraws the strokes already on screen, so you can tune the look against a line you just drew. The color stays in the footer. The highlighter always has a constant width.

The whiteboard#

W (or the Whiteboard button in the footer) covers the slides with a blank page in the deck's background color, dotted to show where it scrolls. Draw on it as on a slide. It is a single page without edges: the arrow keys glide the view by a quarter of its width or height, the mouse wheel or a two-finger scroll on a trackpad moves it directly, and on a touch screen two fingers drag it. Space still moves the presentation underneath, so W again returns to the slides where you left them.

The whiteboard keeps its content while you move through the deck, and its undo, redo and clear are its own: nothing done there touches the strokes on slides. Its content lasts until the page is reloaded.

A tablet as a drawing pad#

A tablet on the same network can show the current slide and let you draw on it with a pencil, mirroring the strokes on the presenting screen as they are drawn, in both directions. The tablet runs none of the engine: it shows a picture of the presenting screen's view, which the presenter sends again whenever a stop settles or the window is resized, and draws the strokes itself as vector ink on top. The page therefore loads at once on any tablet, answers the pencil immediately, and accepts no gesture other than drawing: a pencil or a finger never selects, scrolls or zooms anything.

  1. Serve the deck so that other devices can reach it: zoomdeck serve deck.json --host, or npm run dev -- --host from the checkout. Pairing needs this server; an exported HTML file opened from disk cannot pair.
  2. On the presenting computer, open the settings and, under Tablet, choose "Show the pairing code". A QR code and an address appear: one for the computer's name on the network (Name.local, which finds the computer whatever its address), and one per network address.
  3. Scan the code with the tablet (or type the address): the pad page opens there, with the current slide and a toolbar for the tool (pen or highlighter), color swatches, tip size, undo, redo, clear and the whiteboard, and nothing else. A fingertip erases the stroke it touches. Adding it to the home screen gives it the whole screen.

Coming back without the code. The code admits the tablet for the presenter's session: a temporary connection, for a borrowed tablet, say. A tablet of your own is better trusted: when it opens the pad page without a valid code, a small prompt on the deck says which tablet asks to draw, and offers "Allow once" or "Always allow"; the Tablet section of the settings lists the tablets connected, with a Trust button, and the trusted ones, with Forget. A trusted tablet comes back on its own: the pad page, opened from a bookmark or the home-screen icon, remembers the presenter and is let in without the code, for every deck shown in that browser. The pairing survives a change of the computer's network address when the tablet reached it by name; it needs the same port, so start the server with --port when the default one can be busy. Safari may forget the pad page's memory after a week without a visit, in which case the tablet scans the code once more and is recognized again.

Over a cable. The pairing needs a network between the two devices, not necessarily Wi-Fi. On a Mac, plug the iPad in, open System Settings → General → Sharing → Internet Sharing, share your connection "from" the interface you use (Wi-Fi) "to" iPad USB, and turn it on; the computer gets an address on the cable (192.168.2.1 as a rule), the pairing panel shows a code for it next to the Wi-Fi one, and the tablet scans that one. The cable link is private and steadier than a crowded Wi-Fi. An iPad with a cellular plan can also share its own connection to the computer over USB (Personal Hotspot), which works the same way.

The pad follows the presentation and cannot move it, so a stray touch never changes the slide. Strokes, undo, redo, clear, and the whiteboard with its scrolling are shared both ways; each page draws the shared strokes with its own pen style, so the presenting screen always shows them in the style chosen there. An Apple Pencil hovering over the tablet shows as a red dot on the presenting screen. A graphics tablet without a screen (a Wacom) needs no pairing: plugged into the computer, it drives the pointer and draws with pressure directly.

The tour's chapter Pen, whiteboard and tablet is a test page for all of this: shapes to trace, a strip for the pressure, a box to write in with the palm resting, and the checklist to go through once the tablet is paired.

The pad page needs almost no memory, since the deck itself stays on the computer. Opening the deck itself on a tablet, as a viewer, is also possible when it is small enough: Safari closes a page that uses too much memory ("A problem repeatedly occurred"), so text is rasterized at the same base density on every screen and only the lines in view are sharpened to the screen's density.

The camera#

The camera is yours to turn on. Camera in the footer turns it on in a corner of the slides, or off again; C steps through the three states: in a corner, full frame in place of the slides or the whiteboard (a way to show a real blackboard, an object or yourself without leaving the deck), and off. The first step asks the browser for the camera chosen in the settings (Recording). In the corner the picture is mirrored like a mirror, so that you can see yourself; the corner, the size and the mirroring are settings. The pen never draws on the camera: strokes belong to slides and to the whiteboard. A recording keeps the camera exactly while it is on, so you can record a talk without it even with a camera attached, or bring it in for some moments only. The camera and the screen (see Filming the screen) are one picture at a time: turning the camera on stops the filming of the screen, and filming the screen sets the camera aside, to come back when the filming stops.

Recording a talk#

Record in the footer, or R, records the talk (a dialog first says what will be kept and where it goes, and offers to continue a saved talk instead of starting a new one): your voice through the microphone, the camera while it is on (see The camera; recording never turns it on by itself), and everything you do on the deck, each on the same clock: the stops you move through and how (played forwards, backwards or jumped to), every pen and highlighter stroke as it is drawn, undo and clear, the whiteboard with its scrolling, the laser or spotlight while it is shown, the playback speed and the camera switches. The slides themselves are not filmed. A recording is therefore a few files: the sound or video from the microphone and camera (WebM, or MP4 in Safari; a further file each time the camera is turned on or off, since a picture cannot join a file being written, and when the talk is continued after a stop), and a small timeline of what happened, naming each stop by its slide and step. It plays back on the deck itself, as sharp as the live deck at any window size, and survives edits to the deck that leave its slides in place; the player warns when the deck changed since the talk was recorded.

The header shows the running time while recording. Pause stops both the microphone and the clock, and resuming leaves the gap out of the talk. Stop saves the recording in a recordings/ folder next to the deck when the deck is served by zoomdeck serve or the development page; otherwise the two files are offered as downloads, to put in such a folder by hand. When ffmpeg is installed, the server also rewrites the media file so that it carries its duration and can be seeked in any player. The recording is named after the date and time, talk-2026-11-13-1030.

Filming the screen. A talk may leave the deck for a while: a demonstration in another application, a terminal, a web page. Screen in the footer, or S, films the screen in place of the camera until you press it again; the button is red, and the header says Screen, exactly while the screen is filmed, and the camera, if it was on, is set aside meanwhile and comes back after. The first time, the browser asks which screen or window to share: its picker is the browser's own, which a page can neither skip, fill in nor confirm, though it opens on the kind of surface you chose last time (the entire screen at first; choose that to follow yourself across applications). Stopping the filming keeps the share, so that the next S films the same screen again without asking; the small arrow beside Screen, shown while a share is held, opens the picker again to film something else instead. Ending the share from the browser's own bar (Chrome's "Stop sharing", Firefox's and Safari's sharing indicators) works too, and the next S then asks again. With "Film the screen while this page is hidden" in the settings, a recording asks for the screen when it starts and films it by itself whenever the page is hidden, as when you switch to another application, and goes back to the deck when you return; the switch still works at any time. A filmed stretch is a further media file of the talk; in playback and in the exported video it fills the frame, whole, and the deck stands still meanwhile. Your voice goes on through the microphone, while the sound of other applications is not captured. The browser needs the system's permission to record the screen (macOS asks once), and screen files are larger than camera files, about twenty megabytes a minute.

Continuing a talk. A stopped recording can be taken up again later, even after closing the browser: the Record dialog offers every saved talk to continue, and so does the Continue button in the list of talks. What follows is stamped after the end of the earlier part, with a cut marking the seam, and is kept as a further media file of the same recording (talk.part2.webm, since a browser cannot append to a finished file); playback and the export run through the parts as one talk. The header counts from where the talk stood. Stop and Continue therefore also serve as a pause that outlives the page, and the deck may be edited in between, as long as the slides already visited stay.

Choose the devices first, in the settings under Recording: "Detect devices" asks the browser once for the microphone and camera, after which both lists show the devices by name ("None" for the camera leaves it out altogether). "Test the devices" opens the chosen ones as a recording would and shows what they capture: the camera picture, and a bar that follows the sound level as you speak; "Hear a 3-second sample" records three seconds from the microphone and plays them back, so you hear what the recording will keep. Changing a device while the test runs switches to it, and the test ends when the dialog closes. The same section sets the corner (bottom right by default) and the size of the camera picture, which is where it appears while you present and in the exported video, and whether your own picture is mirrored while presenting (the recording is never mirrored).

Playing a recording. When the deck's folder holds recordings, the footer shows Talk (with their number when there are several); it opens the recording, or a list to choose from, with a Delete button where the server can remove files. The same list opens from Talks… in the playback bar, so one recording can be continued or deleted as well. During playback the deck follows the recording: stops play as they did, strokes appear as they were drawn, the whiteboard and the camera switch when they did, and the laser or spotlight moves where the presenter pointed. The footer gives way to a playback bar with play and pause (also Space or K), a scrubber (also ← and →, five seconds at a time, and Home for the start), the time, Export video and a way out (also Esc); these keys have their own group in the Keyboard settings. Seeking anywhere rebuilds the state at that moment. The recording's own sound plays, and its camera picture sits in the corner it was recorded with, or full frame where the presenter switched to it. Your keys, taps and clicks on the deck, the pen, the whiteboard, the spotlight, links and the outline are off until you leave the recording, so that nothing of yours gets mixed with what the timeline replays.

Exporting a video. Export video renders the open recording as a video file: the slides or the whiteboard with their ink, the camera in its corner or full frame, the pointer, and the recorded sound, at 1920 pixels wide in the deck's proportions. A dialog first asks for the file's name and where it goes: the recordings/ folder next to the deck (with the preview server), a folder of your choice through the browser's own save dialog (Chrome and Edge), or the browser's downloads. The recording plays from the start while the frames are captured, so the export takes exactly as long as the talk, and the window must stay visible meanwhile (a hidden tab draws nothing). The result is a WebM file (VP9 and Opus), saved as recordings/<name>.video.webm next to the recording or downloaded; ffmpeg -i talk.video.webm -c:v libx264 -c:a aac talk.mp4 turns it into an MP4 when a tool or a site wants one. The timeline is independent of the clock that produced it, so a later version can render the same recording off line, faster than real time.

What travels where. The folder export (zoomdeck build … -o folder) copies the recordings/ folder, so a deck published that way shows its Talk button to viewers; the single-file export leaves recordings out, since a talk's media would not fit in a page. Playback needs a browser that can play the recorded file: a WebM from Chrome or Firefox plays in those two, an MP4 from Safari plays everywhere.

Printing to PDF#

Print in the footer (or P, also ⌘P / Ctrl+P) writes the deck as a PDF file, one page per stage: every stop the presentation passes through, in order, as the screen shows it at that stop, with the camera where it rests. Returns to a parent slide after its children, which show nothing new, and stops the presentation runs straight through (automatic advancement, as before a looping movie), seen only as the start of the next, are left out. The dialog offers two choices:

  • Pages: every stage, or only the last stage of each slide, which makes a handout without the intermediate steps. The page counts are shown next to each choice.
  • Resolution: the width of each page picture, 1920, 2560 or 3840 pixels; the height follows the deck's proportions. A 16:9 deck prints on a 960 × 540 pt page (the size of a widescreen slide), so 2560 pixels are about 190 dpi and 3840 about 290 dpi.

The pages are drawn by the deck's own renderer at the chosen resolution, with the text rasterized at that density, so they are as sharp as the screen at rest, and stored losslessly (deflated, as PDF readers decode natively; a browser that cannot deflate gets fine JPEG pages instead). Formulas, shapes, images and code look as they do on screen; pen strokes, highlights, the whiteboard, the spotlight and the camera picture are not part of a page, and an embedded page (iframe:) prints as its frame with its poster or caption. The forest of slides becomes the bookmarks of the file, so a PDF reader's outline lists the slides.

A stop whose animation leaves the picture as it was (an indication, a flash, a full turn along a path) prints as a page that looks like the one before it: it is a stage all the same.

While the pages are made the deck leafs through its stops in the window, at the pace of the renderer (a page takes a fraction of a second; a long deck at 4K, a minute or two); closing the dialog cancels, and the deck comes back to the stop you were at either way. The file is downloaded by the browser, named after the deck's title. Printing is not available while a talk is recorded or played back.

Rendering options#

The stage draws on a WebGL canvas whose context is created once, so the two rendering options in the settings (Advanced) take effect when the page is loaded again; the dialog offers a reload. Antialiasing smooths the edges of shapes and lines with multisampling; it is on by default and is the costliest option on Firefox, whose WebGL on macOS runs on an older graphics stack than Chrome's. Render resolution is the number of device pixels per CSS pixel: the screen's own (up to 2×) by default, 1× for fewer pixels and faster frames, or 2× to supersample a low-resolution screen, which sharpens text and lines there at the price of four times the pixels. The drawing buffer is not preserved between frames, which spares a copy per frame; printing and the video export read the canvas right after a render, so they are unaffected.

Reverse playback#

Going back plays the stop's animations in reverse: fades, property animations, effects, morphs and camera moves retrace their path, including zooming back into a child you just left. Videos, loops and updater-driven waits cannot run backwards and are passed over. Pressing ← again during a reverse settles it and steps back once more, so quick presses step through stops. "presenter": { "reversePlayback": false } restores instant jumps.

Cuts#

"presenter": { "transition": "cut" } replaces the view at once when the camera moves between slides, like turning the pages of a PDF, while fragments and animations inside a slide still play. transition=cut on a slide, include or subslide does the same for that slide alone, and transition=zoom brings the zoom back for one slide of a cut deck. A PDF opened in the player, and a Markdown file whose slides include none of the others, cut between their pages by themselves (PDFs, flat decks and forests).

Outline and deep links#

The outline lists every slide; clicking one jumps to its first stop. The address bar follows the current stop as #step=N, which can be shared or bookmarked; #slide=id opens the first stop of a slide by its markup ID (or a slide by name). A URL without a hash opens at the first stop, and a hash typed into the address bar of an open deck moves it there; only the preview server's own reload after an edit returns to the stop you were looking at.

Loops and automatic advancement#

Looping stops (play=loop, stop({ loop: true })) repeat until the next click. Stops marked with next or autoNext advance by themselves once played; backward moves never trigger them.

Editing preview#

zoomdeck serve and the dev page reload when the deck's files change and return to the slide and step you were on, using a bookmark saved just before the reload. Parse and reference errors are shown with the offending line. The editor and the player also follow each other: zoomdeck jump <file> <line> shows the stop of a source line, and E or Alt + click opens the source of a stop or of an element in the editor named by --editor; Editors explains both and the ready-made setups.

Static exports#

A deck can be exported as a static folder or as one self-contained HTML file that opens from disk; both keep the complete player, including settings and deep links. See Exporting a deck.
